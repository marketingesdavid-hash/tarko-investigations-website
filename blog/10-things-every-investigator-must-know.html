10 Things Every European Investigator Must Know

Good investigative work produces conclusions that are accurate, traceable and defensible. These ten principles help turn information into findings that others can assess and use.

Based on Áron Tarkó’s professional guide · 16 October 2026

Investigations often begin with a simple question: is what we are seeing true? A convincing profile, a company record or a detailed account can create an impression of certainty. The professional task is to establish what the sources actually support and what remains unresolved. Áron Tarkó’s guide offers ten practical reference points for investigators, analysts, journalists and professionals supporting business decisions.

The ten principles
  1. Information has value only when verified
  2. OSINT is the foundation, not the whole investigation
  3. Legal and ethical boundaries belong in the method
  4. Documentation starts on day one
  5. Human sources still matter
  6. Patterns reveal more than isolated facts
  7. Europe is not a single jurisdiction
  8. Digital traces need timely preservation
  9. Timing can determine the value of a finding
  10. Credibility is an investigator’s strongest asset

1. Information has value only when verified

A search result is not evidence. The same incorrect claim can appear on several websites, so sources copying one another do not provide independent confirmation. Check the origin, chronology and whether the material concerns the right person or company.

In practice: Cross-check important claims against independent sources and separate facts from assumptions.

2. OSINT is the foundation, not the whole investigation

Open-source intelligence helps map people, organisations and relationships. Public information can also be incomplete, outdated or misleading. Interpret findings alongside documents, local context and, where appropriate, human sources.

In practice: Define the question before choosing your search tools.

3. Legal and ethical boundaries belong in the method

Professional quality includes considering the purpose, limits and proportionality of information gathering. Technical accessibility alone does not answer whether material should be collected, retained or reported.

In practice: Clarify the objective and applicable rules before collecting information.

4. Documentation starts on day one

A screenshot can quickly lose its meaning without context. Sources, dates, URLs and circumstances make a finding traceable. A clear final report begins with disciplined notes throughout the investigation.

In practice: Preserve originals and keep working copies separate.

5. Human sources still matter

A database may show a connection; a person may explain its background. HUMINT depends on preparation, careful listening and checking what people say. Consider memory, motivation and possible bias when evaluating a statement.

In practice: Record who said what and under which circumstances.

6. Patterns reveal more than isolated facts

A repeated address, a change of director and a sequence of events may seem unremarkable separately. A timeline can bring new questions into view. A connection or correlation, however, does not by itself establish causation.

In practice: Search as carefully for evidence against your hypothesis as for evidence supporting it.

7. Europe is not a single jurisdiction

Cross-border work involves differences in registers, procedures and professional contexts as well as language. An established approach in one country may be treated differently elsewhere.

In practice: Involve a professional with local knowledge when the context requires it.

8. Digital traces need timely preservation

Websites change, posts disappear and accounts close. Archives and third-party references may help, but recovery is never guaranteed. Finding information and preserving it properly are separate tasks.

In practice: Capture relevant material together with its source and surrounding context.

9. Timing can determine the value of a finding

Even accurate information can arrive too late. Identify volatile material, urgent questions and tasks that can wait. Prioritisation should never turn uncertainty into an unsupported claim of certainty.

In practice: Preserve volatile material first, then verify the key claims.

10. Credibility is an investigator’s strongest asset

Clients need useful, accurate findings handled with discretion. Professional trust also depends on explaining uncertainty and the limits of a conclusion. A restrained, defensible report is more valuable than a dramatic narrative.

In practice: Do not promise certainty where the evidence supports only a probability.

One question to ask before submitting a report:
Could another professional follow the recorded sources and understand how I reached this conclusion?
