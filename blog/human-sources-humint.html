When the internet is no longer enough: the decisive power of human sources

The most important piece of information in a case is not always stored in a database. Sometimes it is held by a person who understands it and, under the right circumstances, is willing to share it.

Published September 2026

Modern investigation is often associated with digital tools, databases, social platforms and search techniques. These are indispensable, but OSINT can reveal only what has become publicly accessible. The real reason behind a decision, an informal relationship inside an organisation, the background of a conflict or the exact sequence of events may never appear online.

This is where HUMINT – Human Intelligence becomes important: the conscious, structured and verified use of information obtained from people.

HUMINT is not simply conversation.
Its value lies in knowing whom to approach, what to ask, when to ask it and within what professional boundaries – and then comparing the answer with other sources.

What can a person know that a database cannot?

Public records may show who manages a company, where someone previously worked or which formal corporate links exist. A human source can add context: how a decision was actually made, who exercised real influence, which relationship is merely formal, and what happened between two documented events.

Human information can be especially valuable in internal corporate inquiries, insurance cases, missing-person investigations, suspected fraud, counterparty checks and cases where digital traces are incomplete or deliberately misleading.

A source is not the same as the information

A professional inquiry evaluates the source separately from the claim. A direct witness may still be inaccurate, while a more distant participant may recognise a connection that others miss. Access, motivation, memory, bias and independent corroboration must all be considered.

Trust alone is not a method

Trust is essential, but professional source handling also requires a clear objective, preparation, proportionate questioning, precise documentation and continuous source criticism. The investigator must understand both the circumstances of the conversation and the limits of any resulting conclusion.

Choosing an appropriate professional role and style of communication also matters. It must remain lawful, proportionate and connected to the legitimate purpose of the case. Deception is not a measure of professional skill; the objective is to obtain information safely and in a form that can be checked.

OSINT and HUMINT are strongest together

These methods are not competitors. OSINT helps identify people, construct timelines, prepare questions and verify later answers. HUMINT can explain gaps between public facts, point towards new lines of inquiry and reveal context that is invisible online.

The strongest conclusions usually emerge when independent open and human sources point in the same direction.

Professional HUMINT requires legal and ethical boundaries

Human-source work may involve personal data, confidential statements and vulnerable situations. Purpose limitation, proportionality, data minimisation, confidentiality and careful documentation are therefore essential. Not every obtainable fact should be used, and not every possible method can be justified.

A professional investigator should always be able to explain why the information was needed, how it was obtained, how it was verified and precisely what conclusion it supports.
