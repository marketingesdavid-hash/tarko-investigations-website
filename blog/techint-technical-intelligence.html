TECHINT • Technical intelligence

When technical data becomes actionable information

A device can provide a great deal of data. The real question is what we can reliably establish from it.

A product specification, equipment documentation or a technical examination result can seem straightforward at first glance. Yet it is easy to read more into it than it actually proves. Knowing a device’s stated capabilities is different from establishing how it was used in a particular situation.

The value of technical intelligence therefore also lies in interpretation: what did we observe, under what conditions, and what other source supports our conclusion?

What does TECHINT mean?

TECHINT stands for Technical Intelligence. In its classical military sense, it refers to intelligence derived from examining foreign technical equipment and materiel to understand their capabilities, limitations and operation. It involves more than using tools: analysis of the collected technical information is also part of the process.

In civilian investigations, assessing technical information may support different tasks. The tools and methods depend on the investigation’s objective and the access available. It is therefore worth clarifying what TECHINT means in a particular professional discussion or course.

Data is not the same as a conclusion

The fact that a technical system can do something does not, on its own, establish that it did so in the case being examined.

A simple example: what does a specification prove?

Suppose a business makes an offer based on a technical device’s capabilities. Its documentation suggests that the equipment may be suitable for the task. This does not yet establish whether it is the stated model, under what conditions the manufacturer’s figures apply, or whether the supplier has the equipment and experience required.

A sound investigation separates the manufacturer’s claim, the observable fact and the interpretation. We compare documentation with the information available, record discrepancies and identify unanswered questions.

How does it relate to OSINT and HUMINT?

OSINT uses publicly accessible sources, such as published technical documentation or company information. HUMINT may add context from human sources. Technical analysis can help explain the characteristics of equipment and data.

These approaches may meet in the same case. A technical document may come from an open source, while interpreting it requires technical expertise. Comparing independent sources helps identify claims that can only be partially corroborated.

Three questions to ask about every technical finding

What is the source? Are we looking at an original document, a copy, a direct observation or someone’s interpretation? Under what conditions is it valid? Could the environment, configuration or time of creation affect the result? What can be verified? Which parts are facts, which are assumptions and what information is still missing?

Good documentation is part of the method

If we cannot later reconstruct where data came from and how we reached a conclusion, the result is difficult to assess. Record the source, date, examination conditions and uncertainties. Alongside technical accuracy, clarify the boundaries of access and use.

Professional competence also means recognising the limits of our knowledge: when further measurement, another source or an appropriately qualified specialist is needed.
